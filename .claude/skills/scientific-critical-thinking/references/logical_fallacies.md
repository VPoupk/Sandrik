# Logical Fallacies in Scientific Discourse

## Overview

Logical fallacies are errors in reasoning that undermine the logical validity of an argument. In scientific discourse, they are particularly dangerous because they can lead to incorrect conclusions about the natural world. This reference catalogs fallacies commonly encountered in scientific arguments, organized by type.

**Important**: Identifying a fallacy does not prove the conclusion is false — it proves that this particular argument does not support the conclusion. The conclusion may still be true for other reasons.

---

## 1. Causation Fallacies

### Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
- **Form**: "B followed A, therefore A caused B"
- **Example**: "I took vitamin C and my cold went away in a week. Vitamin C cured my cold." (Colds typically resolve in 7-10 days regardless)
- **Why it's wrong**: Temporal sequence alone does not establish causation
- **What's needed**: Controlled comparison showing the outcome differs with and without the proposed cause

### Correlation Implies Causation
- **Form**: "X and Y are correlated, therefore X causes Y"
- **Example**: "Countries with higher chocolate consumption have more Nobel laureates. Chocolate consumption increases intelligence."
- **Why it's wrong**: Correlation can arise from confounders (wealth), reverse causation, or coincidence
- **What's needed**: Experimental evidence, established mechanism, ruling out confounders

### Reverse Causation
- **Form**: "X causes Y" when actually Y causes X
- **Example**: "Depression causes unemployment" (when unemployment may cause depression, or both may be true)
- **Why it's wrong**: Direction of causation assumed without evidence
- **What's needed**: Temporal data establishing which came first; experimental manipulation

### Single Cause Fallacy (Causal Oversimplification)
- **Form**: "The cause of Y is X" (ignoring multiple contributing factors)
- **Example**: "The cause of obesity is eating too much." (Ignores genetics, environment, hormones, gut microbiome, stress, sleep, socioeconomic factors)
- **Why it's wrong**: Complex phenomena rarely have single causes
- **What's needed**: Acknowledge multiple contributing factors and their interactions

### Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
- **Form**: "X and Y occur together, so one causes the other"
- **Example**: "People who eat breakfast weigh less. Eating breakfast prevents obesity." (Confounded by lifestyle factors)
- **Why it's wrong**: Co-occurrence ≠ causation
- **What's needed**: Same as correlation/causation — controlled evidence

## 2. Generalization Fallacies

### Hasty Generalization
- **Form**: Broad conclusion from too few cases
- **Example**: "We studied 12 patients and the treatment worked in 10. This treatment is 83% effective." (Too small to estimate with precision)
- **Why it's wrong**: Small samples have high variance; results may not replicate
- **What's needed**: Adequate sample size; replication; confidence intervals

### Anecdotal Fallacy
- **Form**: Using personal stories or isolated cases as evidence
- **Example**: "My grandmother smoked her whole life and lived to 95. Smoking isn't that dangerous."
- **Why it's wrong**: Individual cases don't represent population patterns; survivorship bias
- **What's needed**: Systematic data from representative samples

### Cherry-Picking (Suppressed Evidence)
- **Form**: Selecting only evidence that supports a conclusion while ignoring contradictory evidence
- **Example**: Citing three studies that support a drug's efficacy while ignoring seven that don't
- **Why it's wrong**: Gives a misleading picture of the total evidence
- **What's needed**: Systematic review of ALL relevant evidence

### Ecological Fallacy
- **Form**: Applying group-level patterns to individuals
- **Example**: "Countries with higher fat intake have higher cancer rates, so if you eat more fat, you'll get cancer."
- **Why it's wrong**: Group-level correlations don't necessarily apply to individuals (Simpson's paradox may apply)
- **What's needed**: Individual-level data and analysis

### Composition Fallacy
- **Form**: Assuming what's true of parts is true of the whole
- **Example**: "Each ingredient in this supplement is safe, so the combination is safe."
- **Why it's wrong**: Interactions can create emergent properties not present in components
- **What's needed**: Testing of the combined intervention, not just components

### Division Fallacy
- **Form**: Assuming what's true of the whole is true of each part
- **Example**: "This hospital has the best outcomes, so every doctor there is excellent."
- **Why it's wrong**: Aggregate performance doesn't apply to every individual
- **What's needed**: Individual-level assessment

## 3. Authority and Source Fallacies

### Appeal to Authority (Argumentum ad Verecundiam)
- **Form**: "Expert X says Y, therefore Y is true"
- **Example**: "A Nobel laureate says vaccines cause autism, so it must be true."
- **Why it's wrong**: Expertise doesn't make someone correct, especially outside their field; evidence matters, not credentials
- **What's needed**: Evidence and reasoning, regardless of who presents it

### Ad Hominem
- **Form**: Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself
- **Example**: "That researcher is funded by industry, so their findings are wrong."
- **Why it's wrong**: The source of funding is a reason for scrutiny, not automatic dismissal; the data and methods should be evaluated on their own merits
- **What's needed**: Address the evidence and methodology, not the person

### Genetic Fallacy
- **Form**: Judging something solely by its origin
- **Example**: "That study was published in a low-impact journal, so the results aren't valid."
- **Why it's wrong**: Quality of research is independent of where it's published
- **What's needed**: Evaluate the study itself, not its origin

### Appeal to Nature
- **Form**: "Natural = good/safe; artificial = bad/dangerous"
- **Example**: "Herbal supplements are safe because they're natural." (Many natural substances are toxic: arsenic, ricin, botulinum toxin)
- **Why it's wrong**: Natural/artificial distinction has no bearing on safety or efficacy
- **What's needed**: Empirical evidence of safety and efficacy regardless of origin

### Appeal to Popularity (Argumentum ad Populum)
- **Form**: "Many people believe X, so X must be true"
- **Example**: "Most people believe this treatment works, so it does."
- **Why it's wrong**: Popularity doesn't determine truth; many widely held beliefs have been wrong
- **What's needed**: Empirical evidence independent of popular opinion

### Appeal to Tradition
- **Form**: "We've always done it this way, so it must be right"
- **Example**: "This herbal remedy has been used for thousands of years, so it must work."
- **Why it's wrong**: Duration of use doesn't establish efficacy; many traditional practices have been abandoned when tested
- **What's needed**: Controlled testing regardless of historical use

## 4. Relevance Fallacies

### Red Herring
- **Form**: Introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention from the argument
- **Example**: When questioned about side effects: "But think of how many lives this treatment saves!"
- **Why it's wrong**: Both safety and efficacy are important; one doesn't negate the other
- **What's needed**: Address the actual issue raised

### Tu Quoque (Whataboutism)
- **Form**: Deflecting criticism by pointing to someone else's similar behavior
- **Example**: "You criticize our study's methodology, but your last paper had issues too."
- **Why it's wrong**: Others' flaws don't excuse yours
- **What's needed**: Address the criticism on its merits

### Appeal to Emotion
- **Form**: Using emotional manipulation instead of evidence
- **Example**: Showing dramatic patient stories to promote an unproven treatment
- **Why it's wrong**: Emotional impact doesn't constitute evidence of efficacy
- **What's needed**: Systematic evidence, not emotional anecdotes

### Appeal to Consequences
- **Form**: "If X is true, the consequences are bad, so X must be false"
- **Example**: "If climate change were real, it would be devastating for the economy, so it can't be true."
- **Why it's wrong**: The desirability of consequences has no bearing on truth
- **What's needed**: Evidence-based evaluation independent of preferred outcomes

## 5. Structural Fallacies

### False Dichotomy (False Dilemma)
- **Form**: "Either A or B" when more options exist
- **Example**: "Either this drug is a miracle cure or it's completely useless." (It could have modest, context-dependent benefits)
- **Why it's wrong**: Presents an artificially limited set of options
- **What's needed**: Consider the full spectrum of possibilities

### Moving the Goalposts
- **Form**: Changing the evidence criteria after they've been met
- **Example**: "Show me an RCT." *[RCT shown]* "But was it replicated?" *[Replication shown]* "But was it done in MY population?"
- **Why it's wrong**: Endlessly escalating demands prevent any evidence from being sufficient
- **What's needed**: Define acceptable evidence criteria before evaluating

### Begging the Question (Circular Reasoning)
- **Form**: The conclusion is assumed in the premises
- **Example**: "This treatment works because it produces positive outcomes. How do we know? Because it works."
- **Why it's wrong**: No independent evidence is actually provided
- **What's needed**: Independent evidence that doesn't presuppose the conclusion

### Straw Man
- **Form**: Misrepresenting an argument to make it easier to attack
- **Example**: "Scientists say we should only trust randomized trials." (Scientists actually advocate for the best evidence appropriate to the question)
- **Why it's wrong**: Attacks a position that wasn't actually held
- **What's needed**: Address the actual argument as stated

### Slippery Slope
- **Form**: Claiming one event will lead to a chain of extreme consequences without justification
- **Example**: "If we approve this gene therapy, next we'll be creating designer babies."
- **Why it's wrong**: Each step in the chain requires independent justification
- **What's needed**: Evidence for each claimed causal link in the chain

### No True Scotsman
- **Form**: Redefining criteria to exclude counterexamples
- **Example**: "No properly conducted study has shown side effects." (Redefining "properly conducted" to exclude studies that found side effects)
- **Why it's wrong**: Ad hoc exclusion protects the claim from falsification
- **What's needed**: Consistent, pre-defined criteria applied equally

## 6. Science-Specific Fallacies

### Galileo Gambit
- **Form**: "They laughed at Galileo too, so my rejected idea must also be correct"
- **Example**: "The scientific establishment rejected my theory, just like they rejected Galileo. History will prove me right."
- **Why it's wrong**: Being rejected doesn't make you right; for every Galileo, thousands of rejected ideas were simply wrong
- **What's needed**: Evidence for the idea itself, not appeals to historical rebels

### Argument from Ignorance
- **Form**: "It hasn't been proven false, so it must be true" (or vice versa)
- **Example**: "No one has proven that this supplement is harmful, so it must be safe."
- **Why it's wrong**: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence (but is also not evidence of presence)
- **What's needed**: Positive evidence for the claim; burden of proof on the claimant

### Nirvana Fallacy (Perfect Solution Fallacy)
- **Form**: Rejecting a solution because it's not perfect
- **Example**: "Vaccines aren't 100% effective, so there's no point in getting vaccinated."
- **Why it's wrong**: Imperfect solutions can still provide substantial benefit
- **What's needed**: Compare to alternatives (including doing nothing), not to perfection

### Unfalsifiability
- **Form**: Making claims that cannot be tested or disproven
- **Example**: "This remedy works on a spiritual level that cannot be measured by science."
- **Why it's wrong**: If a claim can't be tested, it's outside the realm of scientific inquiry
- **What's needed**: Testable predictions that could potentially be shown wrong

### Naturalistic Fallacy (Is-Ought Fallacy)
- **Form**: Deriving what "should be" from what "is" in nature
- **Example**: "Animals don't use medicine when sick, so humans shouldn't either."
- **Why it's wrong**: Descriptive facts about nature don't prescribe what we should do
- **What's needed**: Separate empirical evidence from value judgments

### Base Rate Fallacy
- **Form**: Ignoring the prior probability when interpreting test results
- **Example**: "The test is 99% accurate and I tested positive, so there's a 99% chance I have the disease." (If the disease affects 1 in 10,000 people, the positive predictive value may be very low)
- **Why it's wrong**: Ignores the base rate of the condition
- **What's needed**: Apply Bayes' theorem; consider prevalence alongside test accuracy

### Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy
- **Form**: Finding patterns in random data by defining the target after seeing the data
- **Example**: Testing 100 subgroups, finding one significant result, and reporting only that subgroup as if it were the hypothesis all along
- **Why it's wrong**: With enough comparisons, spurious patterns are inevitable
- **What's needed**: Pre-specified hypotheses; correction for multiple comparisons; replication

### Prosecutor's Fallacy
- **Form**: Confusing P(evidence | innocence) with P(innocence | evidence)
- **Example**: "The probability of this DNA match by chance is 1 in a million, so there's only a 1 in a million chance the defendant is innocent."
- **Why it's wrong**: Confuses conditional probabilities (transposes the conditional)
- **What's needed**: Apply Bayes' theorem with appropriate base rates

## Detection Strategies

### When Reading Scientific Arguments, Ask:
1. **What is the logical structure?** Identify premises and conclusion
2. **Does the conclusion follow from the premises?** Check validity
3. **Are the premises true?** Check soundness
4. **What's assumed but not stated?** Identify hidden premises
5. **Is there a simpler explanation?** Apply Occam's razor
6. **Would this argument be convincing if the conclusion were different?** Test for motivated reasoning

### Common Patterns That Signal Fallacies
- Emotional language instead of evidence
- Attacking people instead of arguments
- Oversimplifying complex issues
- Claiming certainty where uncertainty exists
- Shifting definitions mid-argument
- Ignoring counterevidence
- Appealing to anything other than evidence (authority, tradition, popularity, nature)
- Making unfalsifiable claims

### Responding to Fallacies
1. **Name the fallacy** specifically
2. **Explain why the reasoning is flawed** in plain language
3. **Identify what evidence would be needed** for a valid argument
4. **Acknowledge that the conclusion might still be true** — just not for the reasons given
5. **Offer constructive alternatives** when possible
