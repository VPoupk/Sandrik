# Scientific Method: Core Principles

## Overview

The scientific method is a systematic framework for investigating phenomena, acquiring new knowledge, and correcting or integrating previous knowledge. It relies on empirical evidence, logical reasoning, and skeptical inquiry.

## The Scientific Process

### 1. Observation
- Identify a phenomenon or pattern worth investigating
- Document observations systematically and objectively
- Distinguish between observation (what happened) and interpretation (what it means)

### 2. Question Formulation
- Derive a specific, answerable research question from observations
- Ensure the question is:
  - **Specific**: Narrowly defined, not vague
  - **Measurable**: Can be tested with available methods
  - **Falsifiable**: Can be proven wrong by evidence
  - **Relevant**: Addresses a gap in knowledge

### 3. Hypothesis Generation
- Propose a testable explanation for the phenomenon
- A good hypothesis:
  - Makes specific, falsifiable predictions
  - Is grounded in existing theory or evidence
  - Specifies the expected relationship between variables
  - Can be operationalized into measurable terms
- Always define both:
  - **Null hypothesis (H₀)**: No effect or no difference
  - **Alternative hypothesis (H₁)**: The predicted effect or difference

### 4. Experimental Design
- Plan a study that can test the hypothesis rigorously
- Key elements:
  - Independent variable (manipulated)
  - Dependent variable (measured)
  - Control variables (held constant)
  - Control group (baseline comparison)
  - Randomization (minimizing bias)
  - Blinding (reducing expectation effects)
  - Adequate sample size (ensuring statistical power)

### 5. Data Collection
- Gather data systematically using predefined protocols
- Ensure:
  - Standardized procedures across all participants/conditions
  - Reliable and validated measurement instruments
  - Proper documentation of methods and any deviations
  - Data integrity and chain of custody

### 6. Analysis
- Apply appropriate statistical methods to test hypotheses
- Key principles:
  - Use pre-specified analysis plans
  - Check statistical assumptions before applying tests
  - Report effect sizes alongside p-values
  - Provide confidence intervals for precision estimates
  - Distinguish between confirmatory and exploratory analyses

### 7. Interpretation
- Draw conclusions that are supported by the data
- Consider:
  - Alternative explanations for findings
  - Limitations of the study design
  - Consistency with prior research
  - Generalizability of results
  - Practical significance vs. statistical significance

### 8. Communication and Replication
- Report findings transparently and completely
- Share data, code, and materials when possible
- Submit to peer review
- Encourage replication by independent researchers

## Critical Evaluation Criteria

### Internal Validity
The degree to which a study establishes a causal relationship between variables.

**Threats to internal validity:**
- **History**: External events occurring during the study
- **Maturation**: Natural changes in participants over time
- **Testing effects**: Repeated measurement changes responses
- **Instrumentation**: Changes in measurement tools or observers
- **Regression to the mean**: Extreme scores naturally move toward average
- **Selection bias**: Systematic differences between groups at baseline
- **Attrition**: Differential dropout between conditions
- **Diffusion of treatment**: Control group learns about or receives treatment

### External Validity
The degree to which findings generalize beyond the study context.

**Threats to external validity:**
- **Sample characteristics**: Non-representative participants
- **Setting specificity**: Laboratory vs. real-world conditions
- **Time-bound effects**: Results specific to a historical period
- **Reactive effects**: Awareness of being studied changes behavior (Hawthorne effect)
- **Treatment variation**: Real-world implementation differs from controlled conditions

### Construct Validity
The degree to which measures actually capture intended constructs.

**Threats to construct validity:**
- **Inadequate operational definitions**: Poor mapping from concept to measure
- **Mono-operation bias**: Using only one measure of a construct
- **Mono-method bias**: Using only one method to assess constructs
- **Hypothesis guessing**: Participants infer and react to study purpose
- **Evaluation apprehension**: Desire to appear favorable affects responses

### Statistical Conclusion Validity
The degree to which statistical inferences are accurate.

**Threats to statistical conclusion validity:**
- **Low statistical power**: Insufficient sample size
- **Violated assumptions**: Statistical test requirements not met
- **Fishing/error rate inflation**: Multiple testing without correction
- **Unreliable measures**: Low reliability inflates error variance
- **Restricted range**: Truncated variability reduces detectable effects

## Red Flags in Scientific Claims

### Language Red Flags
| Red Flag | Concern |
|----------|---------|
| "Proves" or "proven" | Science establishes evidence, not proof |
| "Breakthrough" or "revolutionary" | Overhyping incremental findings |
| "Miracle" or "cure-all" | Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence |
| "They don't want you to know" | Conspiracy framing undermines credibility |
| "100% effective/safe" | Nothing in biology is absolute |
| "No side effects" | All interventions have trade-offs |

### Methodological Red Flags
- No control group or inappropriate comparison
- Small sample with strong claims
- No mention of limitations
- Results that seem too good to be true
- Conflicts of interest not disclosed
- No peer review
- Cherry-picked time periods or subgroups
- Anecdotal evidence presented as scientific proof

### Statistical Red Flags
- P-values clustered just below 0.05
- No effect sizes reported
- Multiple outcomes with no correction
- Post-hoc hypotheses presented as a priori
- Selective reporting of "significant" results only
- Confidence intervals not reported

## Causal Inference Standards

### Bradford Hill Criteria (for observational evidence)
1. **Strength of association**: Larger effects are more likely causal
2. **Consistency**: Observed repeatedly across studies and populations
3. **Specificity**: Exposure specifically associated with outcome
4. **Temporality**: Cause precedes effect (necessary condition)
5. **Biological gradient**: Dose-response relationship exists
6. **Plausibility**: Mechanism is biologically plausible
7. **Coherence**: Consistent with known biology and other evidence
8. **Experiment**: Supported by experimental evidence when available
9. **Analogy**: Similar exposures produce similar effects

### Hierarchy for Causal Claims
1. **Randomized experiment** → Strongest basis for causal inference
2. **Natural experiment / quasi-experiment** → Strong if assumptions hold
3. **Longitudinal observational study** → Can establish temporality
4. **Cross-sectional study** → Cannot establish causation
5. **Case report / anecdote** → Cannot establish causation

## Peer Review Principles

### Purpose
- Quality assurance for scientific publications
- Expert evaluation of methods, analysis, and conclusions
- Identification of errors, gaps, and improvements
- Not infallible — serves as filter, not guarantee

### Limitations of Peer Review
- Reviewers may have biases or conflicts of interest
- Cannot detect all forms of fraud or error
- May favor conventional ideas over innovative ones
- Publication bias affects what gets reviewed
- Time pressure limits thoroughness
- Inter-reviewer agreement is often low

## Open Science Principles

### Transparency
- **Preregistration**: Register hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection
- **Open data**: Share raw data when ethically possible
- **Open materials**: Share protocols, stimuli, and instruments
- **Open code**: Share analysis scripts and software

### Reproducibility
- **Computational reproducibility**: Same data + same code = same results
- **Replicability**: New data + same methods = consistent results
- **Generalizability**: Results hold across different contexts

### Reporting Standards
- Follow field-specific guidelines (CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA, etc.)
- Report all outcomes, not just significant ones
- Clearly distinguish confirmatory from exploratory analyses
- Provide complete statistical reporting (test statistic, df, p-value, effect size, CI)
