The 70M vesting arrangement, and what happened to it

VEST-BENEFICIARY-70M moved its full 70,000,000 RSC into a contract named Vesting (0x8bbB6c4B…ea02) on 2020-09-04. On 2022-01-04 that contract paid out in two directions and emptied: 16,041,666.67 RSC (22.9%) to the beneficiary and 53,958,333.33 RSC (77.1%) back to Treasury 2. That is a vesting termination with clawback of the unvested remainder. The detail that makes it interesting: a second vesting contract (0x4Df3043C…b662, still holding 2,755,068 RSC) names the same beneficiary and has a start date of 2022-01-05 — the day after. This reads as a re-papered grant, not a departure. The beneficiary kept 16.04M, was re-granted a much smaller tranche, still holds 8,090,687, and staked 5,000,000 of it in April 2026.

Two structural findings that carry real risk

(a) On Base, the vault address has no contract code. 0x467564…66d3 holds 3,323,117.53 RSC on Base (~$225k) at an address where eth_getCode returns empty, the account nonce is 0, and sampling historical blocks back to Base block 30,000,000 shows code has never existed there. Those tokens cannot move today. The address is CREATE2-derived — the Ethereum instance was produced by the canonical Safe factory via createProxyWithNonce, and recomputing that recipe reproduces the address exactly — so no private key exists for it and it cannot be an ordinary wallet. It is recoverable: the same factory and singleton are deployed on Base with identical bytecode, so replaying the deployment lands the Safe on top of the tokens, and because the salt commits to the owner set the transaction cannot be hijacked.

(b) On Ethereum, the vault Safe’s transaction nonce is 0. It has never executed a single Safe transaction in its life. No withdrawal path has ever been exercised or demonstrated for the 18,642,533.65 RSC it holds. (Its account nonce is 1, which is simply the EIP-161 default for a created contract — the two counters are different things.)

4. How the stake-to-fund flow actually works

Every single large deposit into the vault follows an identical three-hop choreography, executed within a 3–10 minute window. This is not a user calling stake() on a contract — it is an exchange-style deposit-and-sweep pipeline.

STEP 1 — depositor's own wallet (EOA) │ │ sends RSC to a freshly-deployed, single-use ERC-4337 smart account ▼ STEP 2 — DEPOSIT RELAY (ERC1967Proxy → "SingleOwnerMSCA" implementation) │ one relay per depositor; holds a zero balance before and after │ ├──── small "test" tranche, typically 0.1% ──────► HOT-WALLET 0x7F57d306…10cb │ └──── principal ─────────────────────────────────► STAKE-VAULT 0x467564…66d3 (never moves again)

The relays are ERC-4337 modular smart accounts. Reading EIP-1967 implementation slots directly confirms that every relay on both chains points at the same implementation, SingleOwnerMSCA at 0xD206aC7fEf53d83ED4563E770b28Dba90D0D9eC8 — one uniform factory behind the whole deposit pipeline. They are ResearchHub-generated per-user deposit addresses — the on-chain footprint of the app’s “connect wallet and deposit” button. The routing split is informative: small amounts are swept to the operational hot wallet, large amounts to the cold vault. That is textbook custodial treasury management, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence that user balances here are an internal ledger rather than a contract position.

What this means for the “stake” abstraction

Because yield accrues on any RSC held in a ResearchHub account, there is no on-chain distinction between “staked” RSC and “deposited” RSC. The vault is simply where the large custodial balances are parked. A user’s stake is a database row; the vault is the backing. There is no on-chain enforcement of the yield formula, no lockup, no redemption guarantee, and no way for a depositor to exit without ResearchHub acting for them.

So what did the launch actually attract?

Of the $1.85M ever staked, $195,580 (2.04M RSC, 9.3%) came from outside the ResearchHub perimeter — and essentially all of it arrived on Base. The other 90.7% is the organisation, its treasuries, its grantees and its own multisig signers moving RSC from one pocket into another. On Ethereum the external share is 200,000 RSC — 1.07%, a single depositor who bought on Uniswap. A TVL chart for this product would be measuring internal transfers, not demand.

How this changes the reading

Tier B is compensation, not control. These wallets belong to people paid in RSC by the operating treasury over multi-year periods, who later staked part of what they earned. They are described here as treasury-compensated contributors rather than as insiders, and Tier B is deliberately kept separate from Tier A for that reason.

What does not change is the provenance conclusion. Tier B RSC still originated from a ResearchHub treasury rather than from the open market, so it does not represent external capital entering the token. The distinction matters for characterising the people involved, not for the flow-of-funds arithmetic: Tier A + B remains 19,925,849.65 RSC (90.7%), while the share attributable to control-linked wallets is Tier A alone, 17,574,849.65 RSC (80.0%).

One limit should be stated: RSC's USD price before August 2025 is outside the range the public CoinGecko API serves, so these historical payments cannot be sized in dollars here. The cadence and the RSC amounts are what the classification rests on.

Net taker flow is the wrong test, and it is worth being explicit about why

The window shows cumulative net taker flow of −$112,236 — aggressors sold more than they bought. That is not evidence against a repurchase. A buyer working passive limit orders is filled by aggressive sellers, so their accumulation is recorded as taker selling; if they step the bid up, the price rises while net taker flow stays negative. Rising price on negative net taker flow is the signature of passive accumulation, not its refutation.

Two of the four rally days show exactly this shape — 23 April rose 52.3% on net taker flow of −$117,968, and the largest volume day of the year, 6 June, rose 56.1% on −$3,717. The correct bound is the passive side: takers sold $5,750,630 in this window, so the passive side bought that much. $1.78M is 31.0% of it — well within reach of a single participant.

Two observations about timing survive that correction, and one common argument does not.

  • The deposits do not coincide with the price moves. The four days that produced the rally — 23 April, 30 April, 2 May and 8 May — carry $5.9M of the window's $11.4M volume between them and none has a staking deposit. The largest April deposit, 5,000,000 RSC on 20 April, settled on a $75,195 volume day.
  • That timing argument does not refute an off-market sale. Under such a model the deposit and the buying are deliberately decoupled: a purchaser pays off-market, the treasury delivers tokens it already holds, and any repurchase is worked separately through a market maker. Low-volume settlement days are what that model predicts. What the timing does exclude is the simplest reading — that stakers bought RSC on the open market and deposited it themselves.
  • The rally left no durable level. RSC returned to $0.0687 by 2 August, below where the window started, having round-tripped a 2.4× move. Accumulation on the scale discussed would normally leave a higher floor unless it was subsequently distributed.

The attribution can therefore be neither confirmed nor excluded from chain and tape data. An off-market sale followed by a market repurchase produces an on-chain trail indistinguishable from internal circulation, and a passively-executed repurchase produces a tape indistinguishable from net selling. One adjacent observation cuts against a pure accumulation reading: over the same period 7.92M RSC of treasury lineage moved onto exchanges through dedicated deposit funnels (§8.5) — inventory leaving the treasury toward trading venues, which is the opposite direction to a buyback, though it is equally consistent with supplying a market maker that quotes both sides.

Interpretation

The record establishes that roughly 7.92M RSC of treasury lineage has reached MEXC, Gate and Coinbase through single-purpose intermediate addresses, and that the routing is structured rather than incidental — three separate funnels, one per exchange, each retaining nothing.

It does not establish one-way disposal. The flow is partly bidirectional: distributor 0x620c0738 received 1,788,484 RSC back from MEXC 15, and MEXC-FUNNEL took 329,998 from Gate 1. That round-tripping is more consistent with market-making or exchange-side liquidity operations than with disposal. Reaching an exchange is not equivalent to being sold, and order-book data would be required to distinguish the two. The supportable statement is that treasury-origin RSC is routed onto exchanges in volume, with the purpose unresolved on-chain. One structural observation holds under either reading: the Foundation Safe is the same entity that staked 1,483,316 RSC into the staking vault (§6), so a single treasury lineage supplies both the staked balance and exchange inventory.

These three funnels are not the whole route. Why the differences arise, and what does not change

The three divergences share one root: the hot wallet has a long operating history that predates the staking product by roughly two years, so lifetime turnover and post-launch turnover differ by a factor of about three. A second source of divergence is that not every outflow from the hot wallet is a contributor payment. None of this affects the central findings. The vault total, the 90.7% treasury share, the hot-wallet balance and the ~8M RSC reaching exchanges are all independently confirmed. What the reconciliation changes is the magnitude of ecosystem distribution attributable to the staking period, which on the measured record is materially smaller than reported and close to flat in net terms.

Basis and method

Ledger. All ERC-20 Transfer logs for RSC on Ethereum and Base from block 24,845,606 / 44,494,927 (10 April 2026 00:00 UTC) to block 25,747,535 / 49,926,012 (13 August 2026 17:16 UTC): 544,778 events, read directly from eth_getLogs with no unrecovered block ranges on either chain. Base timestamps are exact — the chain runs a fixed two-second cadence, verified against sampled blocks at zero drift; Ethereum timestamps were read per block.

Pricing. Every transfer is valued at the CoinGecko hourly RSC/USD point nearest its own timestamp. The series is continuous across the period with no gap larger than one hour. Dollar figures therefore reflect the price at the moment each transfer occurred, not a period average.

Provenance. Where RSC changes hands repeatedly, origin is assigned by proportional mixing: an address that spends pays out of its holdings in the same proportions it received them. Exchanges and AMM pools are treated as terminal — RSC entering one is recorded as an arrival and traced no further, and RSC leaving one starts a new origin, so attribution is never laundered through a venue. Where an address spends more than it received inside the period, the shortfall is attributed to its opening balance and labelled as such. The bridge is modelled as a single pass-through account, so cross-chain movement preserves origin.

9.5 What this section does and does not establish

  • Reaching an exchange is not selling. A deposit to an exchange wallet is where on-chain visibility ends. Order-book data would be needed to say whether it was sold, and the companion trading analysis finds exchange net taker flow close to flat over the same window.
  • Proportional mixing is a convention, not a fact about individual coins. Where an address holds RSC from several sources, no on-chain evidence identifies which specific units it spent. The convention is applied uniformly and the gross figures are reported alongside so both readings are available.
  • Opening balances are attributed to their holder, not traced further back. Balances held on 10 April are labelled as such; for the vault depositors, §6 traces them individually, but for the wider population they are simply "held before the period".
  • The 10M transfer into the Foundation Safe has no visible consideration. Whether it was a purchase, a return of loaned inventory, or an internal transfer between accounts of the same operator cannot be determined from RSC transfers alone.
  • The residual between 29.3M and the on-chain total is an inference. Chain data cannot observe a database. That 4.64–5.25M of reported stake volume has no on-chain footprint is measured; that it corresponds to stakes placed against balances already credited on the platform is the reading most consistent with a custodial design, and it is not independently verifiable. The alternative — that the reported figure counts something the chain does not represent at all, such as re-stakes or accrued yield — is equally unfalsifiable here, and only ResearchHub's own accounting can separate the two.
  • Whether a hot-wallet transfer is a stake cannot be read from the chain. A deposit into the hot wallet credits a platform balance; whether that balance was then staked, spent on a bounty or left idle is a database fact. The 2,693,554 RSC figure is therefore an upper bound on hot-wallet staking and a lower bound on hot-wallet deposits.

A second never-moved Safe using the same deposit pattern

0x6D1dFd4B…3497 is a 2-of-4 Safe holding 1,006,756.37 RSC on Base and 563,440.49 RSC on Ethereum. Its entire Base balance arrived in a single minute on 2026-05-14: 100 RSC, then 1,006,656.37 RSC, from EOA 0xe028e57f…e582, which swept itself to zero in the process. Its Base nonce is 0 — like the staking vault, it has never executed a transaction. The test-then-principal shape matches the deposit pipeline in §4, but the principal landed in a different Safe with a different signer set, so this is a separate custody or settlement arrangement rather than part of the 21.97M staked. Flagged because a second untouched million-RSC Safe on the same rails is worth understanding before drawing conclusions about where deposits go.

Reading “from 0x0” correctly on Base

FOUNDATION-COMMUNITY shows 32,601,942 RSC arriving from the zero address across 20 transfers. On Base that is not a mint of new supply — the Base RSC contract is a standard OP-stack bridged token (l1Token() returns the Ethereum RSC contract, bridge() returns the canonical 0x4200…0010 L2StandardBridge), so zero-address issuance there is the bridge crediting tokens already locked in the L1 escrow. Genesis-mint reasoning in §6 applies only to the Ethereum contract, whose supply was fixed at 1B on 2020-08-01. An analysis that treats Base zero-address inflows as new issuance would double-count badly.

Spam-token noise on the vault

Of the 96 token-transfer events into the vault address on Base, roughly 90% are unsolicited scam-token airdrops (Planet, Spell, Crispy, Robots, Nyx, Unicorn, Aurora and others) sent to a visibly wealthy address. One of them is a counterfeit token also using the symbol “RSC”, which sent 1.00 unit on 2026-05-03. Any analysis that filters by symbol rather than contract address will pick up that fake. All figures in this memo filter by contract address.
