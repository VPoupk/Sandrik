Revision 2 — what changed

The complete 2020 token-generation mint has now been recovered (§5), and every depositor traced back to it. Three findings moved the numbers: the Foundation Community Safe’s Base capital is confirmed as bridged directly from Treasury 3; the 650k depositor resolves to a Treasury-1 distribution wallet; and one depositor is confirmed to have bought its RSC on Uniswap. Insider share moves from 87.8% to 90.7% — 98.9% on Ethereum and 44.6% on Base.

The headline finding is that there is no staking smart contract. RSC “staking” is a custodial book-entry: users send RSC to ResearchHub-controlled addresses and the yield (“Funding Credits”) is credited off-chain in the application database. ResearchHub’s own documentation confirms the design — “no lockups, no gas fees”, “principal is revocable”, “RSC held in a ResearchHub account automatically earns Funding Credits”, with yield defined as Your Yield = RSC Emissions × (Your Held RSC / Total Held RSC on Platform). Nothing about that requires — or uses — a staking contract, and none exists on either chain.

The address you identified, 0x467564…66d3, is the destination of every large deposit, so your premise was right in substance. Mechanically it is a Gnosis Safe v1.4.1, 2-of-3, that has received 19 deposits totalling 21,965,651.18 RSC and has never sent anything out. It behaves as an omnibus cold-custody vault for the staking product, not as a staking contract.

The second finding is about who actually staked. 90.7% of the RSC in that vault came from wallets that trace back to ResearchHub itself — the genesis mint, the treasury multisigs, or wallets whose keys sign those same treasuries. The single largest depositor, at 9,999,000 RSC (45.5% of the vault), is the wallet that received 700,000,000 RSC in the 2020 token generation event and is itself a signer on Treasury 1 and Treasury 2. Measured as external capital attracted, the launch drew roughly 2.04M RSC (~$197k), not $1.8M.

The 70M vesting arrangement, and what happened to it

VEST-BENEFICIARY-70M moved its full 70,000,000 RSC into a contract named Vesting (0x8bbB6c4B…ea02) on 2020-09-04. On 2022-01-04 that contract paid out in two directions and emptied: 16,041,666.67 RSC (22.9%) to the beneficiary and 53,958,333.33 RSC (77.1%) back to Treasury 2. That is a vesting termination with clawback of the unvested remainder. The detail that makes it interesting: a second vesting contract (0x4Df3043C…b662, still holding 2,755,068 RSC) names the same beneficiary and has a start date of 2022-01-05 — the day after. This reads as a re-papered grant, not a departure. The beneficiary kept 16.04M, was re-granted a much smaller tranche, still holds 8,090,687, and staked 5,000,000 of it in April 2026.

Two structural findings that carry real risk

(a) On Base, the vault address has no contract code. 0x467564…66d3 holds 3,323,117.53 RSC on Base (~$225k) at an address where eth_getCode returns empty, the account nonce is 0, and sampling historical blocks back to Base block 30,000,000 shows code has never existed there. Those tokens cannot move today. See §7.4 for the proof that this address is not — and cannot be — an externally owned account, and for the exact one-transaction recovery path.

(b) On Ethereum, the vault Safe’s transaction nonce is 0. It has never executed a single Safe transaction in its life. No withdrawal path has ever been exercised or demonstrated for the 18,642,533.65 RSC it holds. (Its account nonce is 1, which is simply the EIP-161 default for a created contract — the two counters are different things.)

4. How the stake-to-fund flow actually works

Every single large deposit into the vault follows an identical three-hop choreography, executed within a 3–10 minute window. This is not a user calling stake() on a contract — it is an exchange-style deposit-and-sweep pipeline.

STEP 1 — depositor's own wallet (EOA) │ │ sends RSC to a freshly-deployed, single-use ERC-4337 smart account ▼ STEP 2 — DEPOSIT RELAY (ERC1967Proxy → "SingleOwnerMSCA" implementation) │ one relay per depositor; holds a zero balance before and after │ ├──── small "test" tranche, typically 0.1% ──────► HOT-WALLET 0x7F57d306…10cb │ └──── principal ─────────────────────────────────► STAKE-VAULT 0x467564…66d3 (never moves again)

A worked example, the 5M RSC deposit on 2026-04-20 (Ethereum, all timestamps UTC):

TimeMovementRSC
05:40:47VEST-BENEFICIARY-70M → relay 0x3b1fddbe…9e3f1,000.00
05:43:23relay → HOT-WALLET test sweep1,000.00
05:46:59VEST-BENEFICIARY-70M → relay5,000,000.00
05:50:11relay → STAKE-VAULT principal5,000,000.00

The relays are ERC-4337 modular smart accounts. Reading EIP-1967 implementation slots directly confirms that every relay on both chains points at the same implementation, SingleOwnerMSCA at 0xD206aC7fEf53d83ED4563E770b28Dba90D0D9eC8 — one uniform factory behind the whole deposit pipeline. They are ResearchHub-generated per-user deposit addresses — the on-chain footprint of the app’s “connect wallet and deposit” button. The routing split is informative: small amounts are swept to the operational hot wallet, large amounts to the cold vault. That is textbook custodial treasury management, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence that user balances here are an internal ledger rather than a contract position.

What this means for the “stake” abstraction

Because yield accrues on any RSC held in a ResearchHub account, there is no on-chain distinction between “staked” RSC and “deposited” RSC. The vault is simply where the large custodial balances are parked. A user’s stake is a database row; the vault is the backing. There is no on-chain enforcement of the yield formula, no lockup, no redemption guarantee, and no way for a depositor to exit without ResearchHub acting for them.

Net equals gross, and that is a structural fact rather than a behavioural one. The Ethereum Safe’s nonce is 0, meaning it has never executed a transaction — a Safe can only move tokens through execTransaction, so zero nonce mathematically forecloses any outflow. Read the “no withdrawals” number as a lock, not as loyalty.

7.4 “Couldn’t the Base address just be a wallet the team controls?” — in plain English

Fair question, and the right one to press on: if that address were an ordinary wallet, someone would hold its password, the 3.32M RSC would be perfectly spendable, and the frozen-funds finding would collapse. Here is why it isn’t, without assuming any prior knowledge of how these networks work.

There are two ways an address can come into existence.

  • A personal wallet. You first generate a secret number. The address is then calculated from that secret — it is essentially the secret’s fingerprint. The secret exists first; whoever knows it controls the address.
  • A program (a “smart contract”, e.g. a Safe). No secret is ever generated. The address is calculated from a recipe: which factory built it, and what settings were fed in. There is no password anywhere in the process, because there is nothing for a password to unlock — the code itself decides who can move the money.

Looking at an address alone, you cannot tell which kind it is. You tell them apart by asking where the address came from — and here we can check, because the Ethereum copy was built in the open. Its construction transaction shows it was produced by the standard Safe factory, using the recipe method. So I took that same recipe — same factory, same settings, same batch number — and recalculated what address it ought to produce. It produced 0x467564…66d3, exactly.

That settles it. The address is the output of a recipe, not the fingerprint of a secret. And this cannot be run backwards: finding a secret whose fingerprint happens to land on a recipe’s output is the same problem as guessing somebody’s password by chance — roughly a 1 in 1048 shot, and the assumption the entire system’s security rests on. So there is no password for this address. Not lost — it never existed.

Why the money is stuck, and why that is fixable

The recipe was only ever run on Ethereum. On Base, the address was worked out but the building was never put up. RSC was then sent to what is effectively an empty plot of land. Nothing can move those tokens because there is nothing there to move them — no code, and no password either.

The fix is simply to run the same recipe on Base. The factory it needs is already deployed there, byte-for-byte identical — I compared both. Same recipe plus same ingredients gives the same address, so the Safe would materialise directly on top of the tokens already sitting there, and its owners could move them. That is one transaction, costing a few dollars of gas.

Two details make this less alarming than it first sounds. It is permissionless — anyone can send that transaction, not only the team. And it cannot be hijacked, because the list of owners is part of the recipe: change who owns the Safe and you change the address, so an attacker’s version would land somewhere else entirely and touch nothing. The only Safe that can ever appear at that address is the intended one, with the original three owners.

So the accurate description is immobile but cheaply and safely recoverable, not “lost”. The finding that survives is an operational one: five months and 3.3M RSC in, nobody has sent that transaction.

The technical version, for readers who want it

Creation tx 0x8c06ea48…7de0, sent by KEY-OMEGA to Safe 1.4.1 SafeProxyFactory 0x4e1DCf7A…ec67, selector 0x1688f0b9 = createProxyWithNonce(address,bytes,uint256) — a CREATE2 deployment. Decoding the calldata gives singleton 0x41675c09…461a, saltNonce = 0, and a 484-byte initializer. Then salt = keccak256(keccak256(initializer) ‖ saltNonce) = 0x30526fee…f381, and keccak256(0xff ‖ factory ‖ salt ‖ keccak256(initcode))[12:] reproduces 0x467564dcce90fb21153adfa69c48a3be235d66d3 exactly. An EOA address is last20(keccak256(pubkey)); a colliding key is a 160-bit preimage problem. Corroborating: the Ethereum code is the genuine 171-byte Safe proxy runtime, not an EIP-7702 delegation (which would start 0xef0100 and would in fact indicate a key-controlled account); and Base has never held code at this address at any sampled block back to 30,000,000. Because the salt commits to keccak256(initializer), the owner set cannot be substituted without changing the resulting address.

Two anomalies worth flagging

A measurement caveat, and a live-drift warning

Inflow minus outflow across the 7,569 indexed Base events gives +558,847 RSC, while the wallet actually holds 323,000.42 RSC. The ~236k gap means the explorer’s index is not perfectly complete for this address — the same class of omission that cost a 350,000 RSC transfer in §2. Treat the Base in/out totals as accurate to within a few percent, not to the wei; the direction and shape of the flows are unaffected.

0x901Fc41a…480F holds 15,845,066 RSC, the largest single balance on Base, but it has initiated only 6 transactions in its life. Address-poisoning against the ResearchHub cluster

BASE-DESK has received three zero-value transfers from 0x198566394a02488e1cf7529d4bcc39b1a7ef5c87 — a vanity address engineered to mimic its genuine counterparty 0x1985EA6E9c68E1C272d8209f3B478AC2Fdb25c87 (same first four and last three characters). This is a classic address-poisoning attack timed immediately after each real transfer, intended to get a truncated address copied from history. Worth flagging to the team as an operational risk.

A second never-moved Safe using the same deposit pattern

0x6D1dFd4B…3497 is a 2-of-4 Safe holding 1,006,756.37 RSC on Base and 563,440.49 RSC on Ethereum. Its entire Base balance arrived in a single minute on 2026-05-14: 100 RSC, then 1,006,656.37 RSC, from EOA 0xe028e57f…e582, which swept itself to zero in the process. Its Base nonce is 0 — like the staking vault, it has never executed a transaction. The test-then-principal shape matches the deposit pipeline in §4, but the principal landed in a different Safe with a different signer set, so this is a separate custody or settlement arrangement rather than part of the 21.97M staked. Flagged because a second untouched million-RSC Safe on the same rails is worth understanding before drawing conclusions about where deposits go.

Reading “from 0x0” correctly on Base

FOUNDATION-COMMUNITY shows 32,601,942 RSC arriving from the zero address across 20 transfers. On Base that is not a mint of new supply — the Base RSC contract is a standard OP-stack bridged token (l1Token() returns the Ethereum RSC contract, bridge() returns the canonical 0x4200…0010 L2StandardBridge), so zero-address issuance there is the bridge crediting tokens already locked in the L1 escrow. Genesis-mint reasoning in §6 applies only to the Ethereum contract, whose supply was fixed at 1B on 2020-08-01. An analysis that treats Base zero-address inflows as new issuance would double-count badly.

Spam-token noise on the vault

Of the 96 token-transfer events into the vault address on Base, roughly 90% are unsolicited scam-token airdrops (Planet, Spell, Crispy, Robots, Nyx, Unicorn, Aurora and others) sent to a visibly wealthy address. One of them is a counterfeit token also using the symbol “RSC”, which sent 1.00 unit on 2026-05-03. Any analysis that filters by symbol rather than contract address will pick up that fake. All figures in this memo filter by contract address.

Provenance and caveats. Every balance, owner set, nonce, supply figure and transfer in this memo was read from Ethereum and Base chain state on 2026-08-02 via archive JSON-RPC and the Blockscout explorer APIs; no aggregator, dashboard or third-party analytics product was used for on-chain facts. USD figures apply CoinGecko’s daily close for researchcoin to the UTC date of each transfer — a daily close, not an execution price, so per-line values carry intraday error, largest around the volatile 24 April and 3 May prints. Wallet nicknames are my own labels describing observed behaviour; they are not ResearchHub designations except where the memo states the address is published in ResearchHub’s documentation. Attribution of wallets to the organisation rests on signer overlap, genesis-mint receipt and treasury funding paths, all of which are verifiable on-chain; no wallet is attributed to any named individual, because chain data does not support that and I have not inferred it. Addresses in the “not established” tier may well turn out to be exchanges or unrelated third parties. This is an initial pass — §11 lists what is still open.
