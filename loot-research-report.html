Key conclusions
  • Loot & More Loot are non-commercial. Neither contract takes a fee, holds ETH, or pays the deployer. More Loot has no supply cap and grows ~262,000 claimable IDs per year, tied to Ethereum block height.
  • Neither item contract can be bricked — not by an attacker, and not by the owner. The owner's only powers are transferOwnership and renounceOwnership; minting has no admin gate.
  • AGLD is the opposite story: it has a discretionary daoMint function with no on-chain cap, used once in Feb 2025 to mint +15.5M (+20%). That mint was DAO-authorized (AGIP-9/10) and executed through a 7-day timelock — but the 96M cap is a social promise, not contract-enforced.
  • AGLD supply is highly concentrated: the top wallet holds 41.5%, top 5 hold 70%, and 12 whale wallets hold 83% of supply.

1 · Loot collection

The genesis collection. 8,000 bags of randomized adventurer gear, generated and stored entirely on-chain. Bags #1–7777 were claimable by the public; #7778–8000 were reserved for the deployer. Every item string is derived deterministically from the token ID — no images, no metadata server, no IPFS.

Item structure

Each bag holds 8 slots: Weapon, Chest, Head, Waist, Foot, Hand, Neck, Ring. Items are drawn from fixed vocabularies and decorated with optional name prefixes (68), name suffixes (18), and order suffixes (16), producing the rarity spread.

How rarity works

Rarity is not a stored attribute — it emerges from how often a string appears across all 7,777 bags. A plain "Wand" is common; a "Divine Robe of the Twins +1" with a name prefix, name suffix, and greatness bonus is vanishingly rare. We computed exact occurrence counts for all 7,098 distinct strings and bucketed them into five tiers.

Supply reality check

Of 2,511,701 currently claimable token IDs, only 136,661 (5.4%) have actually been minted. Anyone can claim an unminted ID right now for gas alone — no fee to the contract, no fee to the deployer. The "2.5M claimable" figure is the size of the open door, not the number of bags in existence.

2.2 · More Loot — The 18,338-Bag Mint Anomaly

Because token IDs are user-chosen, mint density reveals coordination. The 500,000–599,999 range shows a sharp ~3.4× spike above baseline. On-chain analysis confirms this was Sybil minting by a single operator.

The evidence

  • 10 wallets, 10 disjoint 10k slices. Each wallet confined itself to its own block of IDs (500k, 510k, 520k … 590k) with zero overlap — a pattern impossible to produce by independent chance.
  • Single-purpose addresses. Each wallet's transaction nonce roughly equals its More Loot balance, meaning the wallets did almost nothing except mint these bags.
  • ~69% concentration. The 10 coordinated wallets account for the bulk of the 18,338 bags in the range; the remaining ~31% is spread across ~2,400 ordinary wallets averaging ~2.4 bags each.
Interpretation

This is not a contract vulnerability — More Loot is permissionless by design, and minting 18,338 bags cost only gas. It is a behavioral fingerprint: one actor systematically farming a contiguous ID region, likely to claim "round-number" or sequential IDs at scale. It dilutes no one financially (there's no fixed supply) but it does distort any naive "unique minter" count.

Who controls minting today?

Not one individual, not a multisig — the DAO, via on-chain governance. The Feb 2025 mint was executed by an ordinary EOA (0xe715ee…71a9) who merely paid gas to trigger an already-passed, already-queued proposal. That executor had no special privilege; the privilege lives in the Governor + Timelock, controlled by AGLD voters.

But minting was never revoked

daoMint is in AGLD's immutable bytecode and cannot be removed. The timelock owner has not been renounced. With a successful vote and 7-day delay, the DAO can mint again — and there is no on-chain ceiling. The only protection against runaway dilution is the 96M social commitment plus the halving schedule and voting dynamics.

Q&A — Your Specific Questions

The follow-up questions raised during this research, answered directly with the supporting on-chain findings.

Does the More Loot deployer make money from new mints, and what's the maximum that can ever be minted?
No revenue, no maximum. claim() is non-payable and the contract has no receive(), fallback(), withdraw function, or royalty — the deployer has earned $0 across the contract's life. There is no hard cap: the claimable ceiling is block.number / 10 + 1, growing ~262,000 IDs/year indefinitely. Today ~2.51M IDs are claimable but only 136,661 (5.4%) have been minted.
Can the More Loot contract be bricked or blocked from minting?
No. The bytecode contains no SELFDESTRUCT in any executable path, no pause mechanism, no proxy/upgrade slot (code is immutable), and no mint-limit override. claim() carries only a reentrancy guard — no admin gate of any kind.
But can the owner himself brick the contract?
No. The owner's only on-chain powers are transferOwnership() and renounceOwnership() — standard OpenZeppelin Ownable functions that don't touch minting. claim() has no onlyOwner modifier, no pause guard, and no dependency on any owner-controlled variable. Even transferring ownership to a malicious contract or a dead address leaves claim() working identically for everyone.
Can you verify with absolute certainty there's no way to stop minting other than Ethereum shutting down?
The owner-specific conclusion is solid. The broader "absolute" claim carries honest caveats, since this is bytecode inspection rather than formal proof: (1) the reentrancy guard reads a storage slot — no function can set it externally, but a hypothetical exploit that stuck it "entered" would freeze claim() (none found in 4.7 years); (2) a future Ethereum hard fork could change block.number behavior; (3) L1-level censorship could drop claim() txs without bricking the contract. None of these are owner-actionable, and all are remote in practice. Bottom line: the owner specifically cannot brick it; a permanent halt requires a chain-wide or protocol-level failure.
Was the 18,338-bag mint spike one wallet or many?
One operator using 10 single-purpose wallets, each confined to its own disjoint 10,000-ID slice within 500k–599k (zero overlap). Each wallet's nonce ≈ its bag count, proving the addresses existed almost solely to mint. These 10 wallets account for ~69% of the 18,338 bags in that range; the rest is ~2,400 ordinary minters. Permissionless by design — it cost only gas — but a clear coordination fingerprint.
Were there DAO proposals/votes for the new AGLD mint, and who is the minter now — an EOA, a multisig, the DAO, or one person?
The Feb 2025 mint corresponds to AGIP-9 / AGIP-10 (off-chain governance docs confirm the proposals and halving schedule; raw vote tallies weren't machine-retrievable from the JS-rendered UI). On-chain, control is unambiguous: minting authority sits with an OpenZeppelin TimelockController (7-day delay) governed by the Adventure Gold Governor — i.e., the DAO. It is not a single EOA or multisig. Crucially, the founding 11-signer multisig handed control to this DAO timelock on Jan 6, 2025 — 30 days before the mint. The mint's executor was an unprivileged EOA who only paid gas to fire an already-passed proposal.
Has the minting function been revoked since? Is the original 2021 founder still involved?
Not revoked — daoMint is permanent in immutable bytecode and the timelock owner hasn't been renounced; the DAO can mint again (no on-chain cap). On the founder: AGLD was launched by Will Papper in Sep 2021; his deployer EOA is now dormant (3 lifetime txs) and a wallet that received the first-ever claim is one of the 11 founding multisig signers. Loot's own founder (Dom Hofmann) is not connected to AGLD ownership — that address never held AGLD and has a zero nonce. The founders' control group voluntarily migrated minting power to on-chain DAO governance before exercising it.

Data Integrity Note

On data provenance

Per the research directive, every conclusion in this report derives from your questions and directly verifiable on-chain data only — RPC calls to Ethereum mainnet, raw bytecode, contract storage reads, and Transfer-event sweeps. Third-party web sources were used solely for off-chain context (founder identity, AGIP proposal names, exchange-wallet labels) and are flagged as such wherever they appear. Methodology & Sources

On-chain methods

  • Item data: tokenURI fetched per minted bag and decoded; items independently re-derived via the contract's pluck() keccak algorithm to confirm. More Loot IDs enumerated via tokenByIndex (ERC-721 Enumerable) since they're non-sequential.
  • Contract security: full bytecode pulled via eth_getCode; function selectors mapped; proxy slots (EIP-1967) and storage checked via eth_getStorageAt; ownership traced through OwnershipTransferred logs.
  • Supply & holders: AGLD reconstructed from a complete Transfer-event sweep (302,799 events) with balances accumulated per address; supply cross-checked against totalSupply() at multiple historical blocks via binary search to pinpoint the Feb 2025 mint block.
  • Mint trace: the 15.5M flow followed transfer-by-transfer from the mint tx through four Safes to downstream EOAs; selector 0x62759f6c identified as daoMint(uint256) via the 4byte signature database.
