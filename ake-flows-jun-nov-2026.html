What the chain showsA token that rose roughly 449-fold in under four months and then gave back 98.3% of it. Through the entire round trip, 40.6608bn of team-controlled supply was released and 2.0000bn of it was sold — one release, in July, before the rise had even finished. Every other recipient still holds every token, including through the collapse.
A negative result, and it mattersThe thing this page looked hardest for and did not find: team-pool supply hitting the market during the fall. Between 21 September and 12 November the eight pools released 1.3002bn in total, on one day, and not a single token from that release — or from any release since June — has reached an exchange. The 98 wallets holding the 22 July node unlock have not moved either. Whatever drove a 98% fall, it was not these wallets selling.

What the chain showsThe same figures recur across Team Pool 2, the Investors Pool and Team Pool 1 — 0.2362bn on 21 Aug (Team 2), 21 Sep (Team 2) and 23 Oct (Investors); 0.1604bn on the same three days; 0.1178bn on 21 Aug and 21 Sep from Team 2 and then 23 Oct from Team 1. Three separately allocated pools, three different months, the same payout figures, and a brand-new receiving address every single time. Address reuse across all 11 events: 0.
Inference · confidence medium-highA single beneficiary roster is being serviced from whichever pool has tokens available, with a fresh wallet generated for each payment. The amounts are the entitlements; the addresses are disposable. That would explain why identical figures cross pool boundaries, why no address ever repeats, and why the recipients behave identically afterwards — they hold, uniformly, without exception.
What would disprove it: a plausible innocent mechanism that produces identical payout figures across three separately allocated pools while never reusing an address — for instance a published vesting table with those exact tranche sizes, which no Akedo document this project has seen contains. Equally, finding that any two of these addresses have different, unrelated funding sources and independent activity would weaken it.
Correlation onlyThe 23 October pair is the tightest instance: Investors Pool at 08:37:38 and Team Pool 1 at 08:38:30 — 52 seconds apart, eighteen fresh wallets between them, from two pools that have no on-chain relationship other than a common owner.

What the chain showsOf 40.6608bn released, 2.0000bn has reached an exchange — 4.9%. The 22 July node unlock, which is the bulk of the rest, was consolidated from 5,879 claimant wallets into 98 wallets with no prior history within five weeks and has sat there ever since, through both the rise and the fall.
Inference · confidence mediumThe consolidation is the part worth worrying about, not the selling. A distribution that reads as 5,879 independent holders is, five weeks later, under the control of however many keys operate 98 wallets. Nothing about that is illegal or even unusual — node sale participants selling to a buyer would look the same — but it means the supply overhang is concentrated and can move on one decision, which a holder-count figure would hide.
What would disprove it: evidence that the 98 wallets are independently controlled — different funding sources, different gas payers, divergent behaviour. So far they behave identically, which is what one operator looks like, but identical inactivity is weak evidence.

The names at the top of the list are aggregators and routers — OKX Labs, the Binance Wallet router, PancakeSwap itself, 1inch-pattern addresses — and one bot, 0x5e2dc2ee…, which has been running the same strategy since September.
Inference · confidence highThe on-chain market did not break the price; it followed it. Two million swaps that net out to four hundredths of a percent of their own flow, with the largest participants being aggregators intermediating between venues, is the signature of arbitrage keeping a thin pool in line with a centralised price that was falling somewhere else.
What would disprove it: a large directional seller hidden inside aggregator flow — the aggregator addresses net out the trades of many users, so a single determined seller routing through 1inch or OKX would appear here as intermediary volume. Attributing that would need the aggregators' own routing data, which is not on-chain.
What the chain showsNone of the largest net depositors to exchange custody over October and November is a pool recipient or a member of the 98-wallet consolidation set. They are Gate.io and MEXC user wallets and unlabelled addresses — the ordinary holder base. The one familiar name is the programmatic drip-seller that has been feeding uniform tranches into Binance Hot Wallet 4 since the summer, and it is still running at the same clip.
A negative result, and it mattersA fall this size with no team-pool selling in it is the kind of result that invites a second look, so: the test is not "did a pool release during the fall" — one did, on 23 October — but "did any released token reach a venue where it could be sold". The answer across every release since June, re-checked at the head block, is no, with the single July exception. If team-controlled supply was sold into this collapse, it was not sold through any wallet these releases created.

9 · Team-adjacent wallets — established, correlated, inferred

The question this section exists to answer is which wallets are plausibly connected to the team, and on what basis. It is kept in three tiers deliberately, because the three are not the same kind of claim and conflating them is how on-chain analysis goes wrong.

The gas trail — ten wallets, one funder, twenty-six seconds

Release recipients are almost all receive-only: they hold no BNB and have never sent a transaction, so there is normally nothing to follow. The 26 July cohort is the exception, because those ten wallets had to call userWithdraw() themselves, which meant someone had to give them gas first. Bisecting eth_getBalance finds the exact block each was funded, and the result is not ambiguous.

What the chain showsAll ten wallets that took the 26 July release — 9.5400bn across five pools — were funded with exactly 0.002000 BNB each, from the same KuCoin withdrawal address. Eight of the ten landed inside a 34-block window, about twenty-six seconds, at 13:48 UTC. The other two came in a single block that morning. Each wallet then called userWithdraw() once, spent that gas, and has never transacted again.
Inference · confidence highThis is one operator. Exchange withdrawal addresses serve many customers, so sharing one is not by itself a link — but ten customers do not withdraw an identical 0.002000 BNB from the same exchange within twenty-six seconds and then each claim from the same set of vesting contracts within the hour. The simplest reading is a single party, operating one KuCoin account, batch-funding ten wallets it controlled in order to execute a release that required per-wallet transactions.
What would disprove it: evidence of ten distinct KuCoin accounts behind those withdrawals, which is not visible on-chain and would have to come from the exchange. That is the one thing that would overturn it, and it is exactly the thing this method cannot see.
What the chain showsThe pattern repeats in miniature on 27 August. The only two wallets from the 21 August cohort that ever moved anything were funded that afternoon — 0.000490 BNB each, from Binance Withdraw_59 and Binance Withdraw_75 — at 17:28:22 and 17:58:38, thirty and three minutes before they each sent their tokens on. Again: funded immediately before acting, by an exchange withdrawal, in an amount sized to the gas required and nothing more.
Correlation onlyEvery other cohort — 3 September, 21 September, 23 October, and twelve of the fourteen from 21 August — holds no BNB at all and has never sent a transaction. They cannot move their tokens without being funded first, which means the moment any of them is funded is an advance signal that something is about to happen with that balance.

Tier 2 — correlated, with the correlation stated exactly

Correlation onlyThe recurring payout amounts. 8 amounts recur across Team Pool 2, the Investors Pool and Team Pool 1, over four release days, to 27 distinct addresses, with zero reuse. This is a numeric coincidence of a kind that does not happen by chance at this precision — but it links the payments, not the people. It does not tell us who the beneficiaries are.
Correlation onlyThe 23 October timing. The Investors Pool's computed vesting boundary fell on 18 October 2026 at 10:00 UTC. The release came five days later, from the Investors Pool and Team Pool 1 together. A release near a boundary is what a schedule would produce; a release five days late, from two pools at once, 52 seconds apart, and paid to fresh wallets, is what an administrative push would produce. Both readings fit.
Correlation onlyReleases follow strength. Seven of eight releases came after a positive seven-day run. Over a window in which the token rose twentyfold, that is close to what chance produces, and eight observations cannot separate the two. It is recorded because it is true, not because it is evidence.

Tier 3 — inference, each with what would disprove it

Inference · confidence medium-highOne beneficiary roster, serviced from whichever pool has tokens. The recurring amounts crossing three separately allocated pools, combined with total absence of address reuse and uniform post-receipt behaviour, is most simply explained by a single list of entitlements paid through disposable wallets.
What would disprove it: an Akedo document setting out tranche sizes that match these figures, which would make the repetition a published schedule rather than a private roster; or two of these addresses showing genuinely independent funding and activity.
Inference · confidence mediumThe 22 July node unlock is concentrated, not distributed. 5,879 claimant wallets became 98 holding wallets within five weeks, and those 98 have not moved a token through a 98% round trip in the price.
What would disprove it: evidence that the 98 are separately controlled. Identical inactivity is consistent with one operator and also with 98 people who simply have not sold, and nothing here separates them.
Inference · confidence mediumThe one sale was positioning, not treasury liquidation. Tokens released in June, moved twice, and sold 34 days later into an eightfold move, for a round two billion.
What would disprove it: a routine explanation for the hop chain — OTC settlement, custodial migration, exchange onboarding — any of which would produce the same trace.
A negative result, and it mattersWhat is not established, and should not be asserted. That any release recipient is the team. That the 98 consolidation wallets are team-controlled. That any release was timed to the price. That the collapse involved team selling. Each of those has been looked for in this data and none of them is supported by it. The honest summary is narrower and more interesting: a single payout roster is being paid across three pools through disposable wallets, and the people receiving it have sold almost nothing.

10 · Method, and what this does not show

How these numbers are made

  • Chain first. eth_getLogs, eth_call, eth_getStorageAt and eth_getCode against a BSC archive node. Prices come from CoinGecko and, independently, from pool slot0 state.
  • Pool flow is complete. Every AKE transfer with a pool on either side, from the token's deployment to block 133,179,531, de-duplicated on (block, logIndex). The segments are checked to tile the chain with no gap before anything is written.
  • Net, and reconciled. Allocation minus net released must equal balanceOf at the head block. All eight pools do, to the wei, across 8 release days and 63,800 lifetime pool-touching transfers.
  • Two prices. Where a dollar figure matters it is derived twice — from the AKE/WBNB and WBNB/USDT pools' own slot0 at that block, and from the CoinGecko hourly series interpolated to the same second. Both are shown.
  • Following forward. "Still held" is balanceOf at the head block. "Sold" means the tokens reached a labelled non-Binance exchange custody address, through as many hops as it took — a lesson from an earlier version of this work, which stopped at one hop and missed the July sale entirely.

What it does not show

  • Who anyone is. Fresh addresses receiving a scheduled payment from a team-controlled pool is what the chain shows. Whether they are employees, investors, market makers or the team is not on-chain.
  • Order books. The great majority of volume happens on exchanges and leaves no on-chain record. Where this page says a move was not on-chain driven, that is a statement about what the chain can account for.
  • Exchanges this registry does not cover. Several venues carrying real volume have no custody address here, so deposit totals are a floor, not a total.
  • Intraday extremes before October. Daily highs and lows for most of this window are derived from an hourly series, which cannot see a spike that opens and closes inside the hour; the last month uses true candles. Where a figure matters, the finer source is used.
  • Intent. Sequences are reported as sequences.
